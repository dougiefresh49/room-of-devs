A.0 THE YARD — every room in the fleet

One idea, stated once: a room is a berth in the yard. The console is what you see standing inside one machine; the yard is the ground you walk between them. The starmap framing is discarded: stars are pretty and unowned, and this room-of-devs is a shipyard, not a sky. Berths are bolted down, numbered, powered or cold, and only one of them has the floor.

DESIGN NOTE — WHY A YARD AND NOT A STARMAP

The starmap placeholder implied rooms floating at distances, which collides head-on with the plot's existing meaning: on the LONG-RANGE PLOT distance already means salience. A second spatial map where distance means "some other project" teaches two different distance grammars in one product. The yard uses adjacency, not distance: berths sit side by side, equally near, and the only number on them is the same salience number at berth magnification. A blip is a node is a face gains a fourth magnification: a berth is a room is a manifest.

Everything on a berth plate is a shrunk instrument you already know: the mini spine spur (bosses lit live / dim queued / green settled) is the rail at 1/8 scale; the LED bar with the red notch is the dock strip's; the lead face in its CRT is the faceplate. Nothing new was invented to be learned — the yard is the console's own parts, rearranged.

DESIGN NOTE — CROSS-ROOM SALIENCE WITHOUT OPENING ANYTHING (THE MUSTER STRIP)

The architecture says the salience queue is shared across rooms with per-room attribution. The muster strip renders that literally: one queue, ascending, room code stamped on every row. It is not per-room summaries stitched together — it is the daemon's own ordering, printed. That is why the answer to "which room needs me" never requires opening a room: the top-left row is the answer, and it carries its own reason line ("PLAN HELD 06:41") and its own gate marker.

The strip is also the only place the gate applies globally. Each berth's LED bar shows its own room's notch; the muster's red rows are the ones below the gate anywhere in the fleet — which is exactly the set Mikey is allowed to speak about unprompted.

DESIGN NOTE — THE AUDIO FLOOR IS A FLOOR

"One global audio floor" is a constraint that usually hides in a mutex. Here it is a hazard-striped bar bolted along the bottom of the yard, with the face of whoever has the floor, an elapsed counter, and the rooms waiting for it. Two honesty wins: you can see why the other room is silent (not broken — queued), and the "one room speaks at a time" rule stops being a surprise. The pun is load-bearing: holding the floor is exactly what it is.

DESIGN NOTE — ATTRIBUTION IN THE PERIPHERAL VISION

The dock strip is ruler-width, so multi-room cannot cost it any width. It gains exactly one thing: a three-LED room spur (one lamp per room, lit = powered, red = below gate) and a room code prefixed to the ticker. The LED bar keeps showing the room you are coupled to; the ticker is fleet-wide. So the rule reads: the bar is where you stand, the ticker is what the fleet is shouting. Clicking a room lamp couples to that room — the smallest possible switch, from the smallest possible surface.

A.3 THE COMMISSIONING BENCH — starting a new project

A room is a manifest file. So the new-project flow is not a wizard — it is a bench where you machine one part and then bolt it into an empty berth. One screen, no steps, no "next". The three dials are real detented knobs; every knob prints its consequence in money and process underneath, because the dials are the only place a person can accidentally make a project expensive. Try the CEREMONY knob — the preview and the manifest change with it.

DESIGN NOTE — THE FLOW'S OUTPUT IS A FILE YOU CAN READ

Every control on the bench edits the manifest pane live, and the pane is not a summary — it is the file that gets written, path and all. This kills the usual new-project anxiety ("what did that wizard just configure?") and matches the architecture exactly: rooms arrive as configuration. It also means the flow has a free second entrance — hand-edit the manifest — and a free undo: git checkout.

The dials carry a consequence line each, in the units that actually bite: ceremony in extra turns, voice in per-character billing, brain tier in dollars per turn. That is the same cost-honesty thread THE CORE runs on, applied at the moment the cost is chosen instead of the moment it is discovered.

DESIGN NOTE — ONE-OFF vs FULL CEREMONY IS THE SAME KNOB, NOT A DIFFERENT FLOW

Turning CEREMONY to BARE does not hide fields — it changes the shape of the thing you are making. The spine line in the manifest goes to none, the preview flips from berth to skiff, and the commission key relabels to LAUNCH SKIFF. A one-off has no berth because it has no manifest worth keeping; it renders in the skiff lane as a dashed diamond and it says out loud that it dies on delivery. There is exactly one place in the product where "is this a real project?" is answered, and it is a knob with three detents.

DESIGN NOTE — WHAT MULTI-ROOM COSTS THE PHONE

Almost nothing, because the phone already renders the muster's job. GLANCE's salience strip becomes fleet-wide (worst row in the fleet, room code stamped), and the thread rows gain a rooms block above them. There is no yard on the phone and no berth plate — the field radio answers "which room is on fire and can I unblock it", not "show me the shipyard". The plot on GLANCE stays single-room: it is the coupled room's craft, exactly as today.

WIRE NEEDS — WHAT PROBLEM A ADDS TO types.ts

Today RoomState is one implicit room. The yard needs the model to grow a level, additively:

Room — {id, code, name, repo, spine: string|null, gearDefault: "bare"|"light"|"full", cast: {lead: PersonaId, alts: PersonaId[]}, brainFloor, connectors: string[], powered: boolean}. This is the manifest file, parsed. spine: null is what makes a skiff a skiff.

FleetState — {rooms: Room[], coupledRoomId: string, muster: MusterRow[], audioFloor: {holderRoomId: string|null, elapsed: string, queue: {roomId, reason}[]}}. MusterRow = {roomId, craftId, callsign, persona, salience, reason, belowGate: boolean} — the daemon's own ordering, not a client-side sort across rooms.

Per-room rollups the berth plate reads: Room.salience (min over its craft, same 0–100 scale), Room.craftCount, Room.heldCount, Room.planDocks: ("live"|"queued"|"settled")[] for the spur bosses. Existing Craft gains roomId; existing RoomState becomes the coupled room's state and everything already built keeps working untouched.

Note what is not needed: no cross-room plot geometry, no per-room threshold (one gate, fleet-wide), no second audio path. salienceThreshold stays a single config number.

§2.6 HONESTY — WHAT MOVES ON THIS BOARD AND WHY IT IS ALLOWED

Moving parts in the yard: the beam on a powered berth's spur (a real fact — that room has a live plan), the waveform + talk lamp on the room holding the floor (a real fact — audio is playing), the red LEDs below the gate (a real threshold crossing), and the CRT scanlines/sweep (pure ambience). Cold berths have no beam. The SKIFF-06 "SCRAPPING…" row is a state, not an animation. Nothing on the commissioning bench animates except the text caret and the needle transition on a knob you turned — both driven by your own input.

B.0 THE CATALOG — the parts book for the machine

Not a gallery. A parts catalog from a machine shop: every component has a part number, a test bench it is running in, a foundry stamp saying what it was cast from, a fitted-on list, and a stock lamp. The browsing model follows the two questions you actually have at 11pm — "what have I got?" (category rail) and "where is this thing already used?" (FITTED filter) — so search is a filter bed, not the front door. It replaces packages/ui/gallery/ and absorbs its job: it lives at /catalog on the prototype (port 5180), one more deck route beside /console and /field, so it ships with the thing it documents.

DESIGN NOTE — THE PROVENANCE CHIP IS A FOUNDRY STAMP, AND IT IS COLOR-CODED BY LINEAGE

Four stamps, four visual families, so provenance is legible without reading: amber + bold = FULLY CUSTOM (ours, the product), blue = a Radix behavior underneath, violet = CVA-only (styling variants, no behavior), green = a third-party library that isn't shadcn (react-markdown, sonner). Amber is reserved for our own metal deliberately — the catalog should make it obvious at a glance how much of the RIG is ours, because that is the maintenance bill.

Where a registry component was considered and rejected, the card prints the rejection as a second, dimmed stamp ("NEAREST: @shadcn/kbd — rejected"). A parts book that only records what you chose gets re-litigated every six months.

DESIGN NOTE — THE STOCK LAMP TURNS THE CATALOG INTO A MAINTENANCE INSTRUMENT

Green = healthy (2+ consumers), amber = single consumer (fragile — one deletion from dead), red = dead stock, 0 imports. The lamp is the same LED as everywhere else in THE RIG, so the catalog reads as an instrument panel for the codebase rather than a docs site. The bins on the rail mirror it: NOWHERE (3) is a bin you can click, and the DEAD STOCK drawer is hazard-striped because unused vendored code is a liability, not inventory.

The consumer counts and file paths are not hand-maintained prose — they come from the audit's own detector (grep -rn 'from "@room/ui"') baked into a generated catalog.json. The page renders that file, so the lamps cannot drift from reality without the file drifting first.

DESIGN NOTE — WHY CATEGORY RAILS BEAT SEARCH-FIRST HERE

27 parts is a shelf, not a library. Search-first UIs assume you know the name; the rail assumes you know the job — and the second question ("where is this fitted?") is genuinely a filter, not a query. So: rail picks the bin, FITTED chips cross-cut it by surface, and the search bed is a live filter over name, part number, base, and path. One spec sheet opens in place at the top of the grid (never a modal, never a new route) so you can compare an open part against the grid behind it.

WIRE NEEDS — none. This is the one surface with zero daemon dependency.

The catalog renders components + a generated provenance file, nothing from PanelSnapshot and nothing from types.ts. Test benches drive components with local fixture props, which is also why every state can be shown at once — the rack is not waiting for a room to enter that state. Generated shape: {partNo, name, bin, base: "custom"|"radix:<x>"|"cva"|"lib:<x>", rejected?: string, file, consumers: string[], surfaces: string[], props: {name,type,note}[]}.

§2.6 HONESTY — a catalog is allowed to animate what the component animates

Every moving thing in a test bench is the component's own real animation (Waveform's bars, Led's pulse, Odometer's roll, the CRT sweep) shown at its true rate — that is the spec. Nothing on this page simulates data: the DialGauge rack shows four fixed fractions, not a fraction climbing. A gauge that animates on a catalog page teaches a lie about the component.

C.0 THE SERVICE PLATE — why is that thing there?

The architecture map is 569 lines of prose that answers four questions per surface. So the visual version is not a graph — it is a service plate: the exploded assembly diagram riveted inside the access hatch of a real machine. Three strata, left to right: what you touch (surfaces) ▸ what it is (the layer stack) ▸ what it runs on (the wire, as a terminal strip). Click any part in any stratum — the plate dims, the ties light, the terminals it energizes turn amber, and the inspector prints the doc's own four questions. The doc is the data; the plate is the renderer.

DESIGN NOTE — WHERE IT LIVES: A ROUTE, NOT A ZOOM RUNG

The zoom ladder is spatial — YARD ▸ PLOT ▸ RAIL ▸ NODE are all views of the same running room at different magnifications. The plate is documentation about the machine, not a magnification of it, so putting it on the ladder would corrupt a contract that is finally clean. It gets its own route, /plate, opened two ways:

1 · From the control deck — one more trigger row, "OPEN SERVICE PLATE", since the deck is already the out-of-world drawer. 2 · Deep-linked from any surface: every housing in THE RIG already has a part number stencilled on it (S-06, F-03…). Shift-click any housing on the console and the plate opens with that part selected. That is the interaction that earns the whole thing — the console becomes its own index, and "why is that thing there?" is answered from where you asked it.

DESIGN NOTE — HOW MUCH DIAGRAM, HOW MUCH INSPECTOR

Roughly 70/30, and deliberately not a node-and-edge graph. Free-floating graphs of 33 parts become hairballs, and a hairball is exactly the failure mode of the 569-line doc restated in another medium. Three fixed columns give every part a permanent address, so muscle memory works: surfaces on the left, the machine in the middle, the wire on the right. Edges are rendered by lighting, not by lines — select FACEPLATE and the tied parts light while everything else drops to 20%. One selection at a time, no multi-select, no dragging: this is a plate bolted to a machine, not a whiteboard.

The middle column is the one place that IS drawn as a diagram, because the layer stack is genuinely a vertical order — voice above interpreter above spine above threads above silent workers — and that order is load-bearing (it is why Mikey survives /clear). Drawing it as a cross-section with dashed conduit between strata teaches the ordering for free.

DESIGN NOTE — THE TERMINAL STRIP IS THE BEST PART, AND IT IS ALSO A PLANNING TOOL

Rendering types.ts as a screw-terminal block — one labelled screw per field, LIVE vs WISH stamped, phase tag on the wish ones — turns the wire wishlist into an instrument. Two things fall out for free: selecting a surface shows exactly what it costs the daemon, and selecting a terminal (yes, terminals are selectable too) shows every surface that goes dark without it. That second direction is the phase-planning question — "what does P3 unblock?" — answered by clicking a screw.

DESIGN NOTE — THE PLATE SHOWS ITS OWN HOLES

The doc ends with a "Not yet designed" section; the plate ends with two red rivets in the middle column. A map that only shows finished parts quietly lies about the machine. Rivet 1 (all-projects view) is what board A fills; rivet 2 (CORE vs GAUGES telling one spend story two ways) is still open. When a hole is filled the rivet goes amber and joins the plate — the gaps list is a UI state, not a maintenance chore.

WIRE NEEDS — one static file, no snapshot

Like the catalog, the plate reads nothing live: plate.json = {parts: [{id, partNo, stratum, group, title, see, why, tie, ties: string[], terms: string[], src}]} plus terminals: [{field, status: "live"|"wish", phase?}]. The one thing worth wiring later is the stratum-I lamp: a part's LED could show whether that surface is currently rendering in the coupled room (faceplate lit, verb rack dark because no verbs are saved). That would make the plate a live x-ray of the room instead of a diagram — but it is a P-late nicety, not day one.

§2.6 HONESTY — the only motion here is your own click

The plate has no ambient data animation at all: no flowing conduits by default, no pulsing nodes. Selection lighting is driven by your input, the dashed layer links between strata are static, and the CRT texture is ambience. The single exception is the stratum-I LEDs, which use the standard LED pulse only where they mirror a real snapshot fact (that surface is rendering / that room is speaking). A map that shimmers is a map that is lying about having data.
