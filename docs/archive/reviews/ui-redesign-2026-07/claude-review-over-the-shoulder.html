Room of Devs · concept B · what I'd do instead

Over the Shoulder
the room isn't furniture

The four boards designed a room — floor, table, tape — and it's a good room. I'd keep the floor and the tape and throw out the table, because the table is a canvas for features that don't exist yet, and meanwhile the thing the room is actually talking about is already on the machine: the work. Three changes follow from that, and one of them is the thing you said is missing.

01 — The unit is the interrupt, not the utterance. All four boards design cutting in as an audio operation. The hard part isn't ducking the speaker, it's whether your sentence reaches the agent in time to change what it does.
02 — The object on the table is the diff. Not a napkin. What Donnie is touching right now — files, diff, last command, current tool call — is data your daemon already tails. Make the centre useful today; glance and sketch become extra sources on the same surface later.
03 — The phone is the product; the Mac is the echo. Every board orders it Mac → dock → phone, then admits the couch is where this tool wins. The Mac already has a great UI for watching an agent work. It's called a terminal.

kept from the fourThe Floor (one voice, ever) · the Tape (turns as records; replay is seek) · ON AIR is achromatic and your mic inverts the surface · the politeness ladder and its sound tokens · lamp-plus-a-word · jersey-only persona hue · confirm-as-a-sentence · the receipt · the bare-<img> avatar contract. Those are settled. Everything below is where I diverge.

01 · the core

Interruption has three outcomes and the UI must never guess

You told me the live call is "mostly just the agent speaking and I listen." The talk button already exists, so the gap isn't input — it's that your sentence has three completely different fates and right now you can't tell which one you got. A room of devs is a place where you can say "wait, don't do that" and be heard before it happens. That is the whole product, and no board designed it.

THE RECEIPT FOR CUTTING IN · the same rigour the four boards demand of the voice interpreter, applied to the thing that actually costs you something. A tool that lets you believe you steered when you only queued is worse than one with no talk button.

the engineering, namedSteer needs the daemon to know whether the target session is between tool calls — which the transcript tail already tells you (live-tail.ts sees tool-use boundaries). Steer = inject on the boundary. Stop = the session's own interrupt. Aside = hold and inject at the next prompt. If the boundary is unknowable for a given agent, the product says "aside" and means it. Never promise steer you can't deliver.

Everything else on all five boards is a way of watching. This is the only part that's a way of working with.

02 · the centre

The Shoulder — the object is the work

Every board puts a shared canvas in the middle of the main window and justifies it by the four futures it unlocks. But glance, napkin, huddle and paste are all unbuilt, so on day one that canvas holds a summary and an empty shelf. Meanwhile the question you actually have thirty times a day — "what is he doing right now?" — is answered by data the daemon already reads: tool calls, file paths, diffs, command output, all sitting in the transcript you tail for live mode.

So the centre pane isn't a canvas waiting for a feature. It's a live view of the selected agent's work. Glance, sketch and paste land here later as additional sources — the surface is the same, but it's load-bearing from day one instead of day two hundred.

03 · instead of ink

Pointing beats drawing

"Ink timed to speech, and it rewinds when you scrub" is the most beautiful idea on the four boards and the most expensive: it needs the model emitting stroke lists with clause anchors, Gemini preserving those anchors through the character rewrite, ElevenLabs word-level timestamps, and a canvas replay engine on the audio clock. Four boards present it as a motion budget.

Take 10% of the machinery for 80% of the payoff. Agents already say "in session.ts:41" constantly — they do it unprompted, in every model, for free. So: the daemon extracts anchors from the raw text before the Gemini rewrite (file:line, a diff hunk, a step in a fenced diagram), attaches them to the turn, and the work pane highlights the anchor while that clause plays. Scrub the tape, the highlight moves with it.

What you keep

The actual insight — that a spoken sentence should have a visual channel, and that scrubbing the audio should move the picture. Same feeling, same "he's showing me while he tells me."

What you drop

Freehand stroke capture, per-stroke timing, a replay canvas, word-level TTS timestamps, and asking a text model to be an illustrator. Clause-level granularity is plenty — you only need to know which line, not which millisecond.

and when you do want to drawYour ink stays: circle a line in the work pane and talk, and the circle plus the voice land as one turn — that half is cheap because you are the one drawing, in real time, with a real clock. Agents point; you draw. That asymmetry is honest and it's how it works with a human colleague anyway.

04 · whiteboarding

The whiteboard is a file

You named whiteboarding as an unbuilt goal, and all four boards answered with a freehand canvas. For a room of coding agents that's the wrong medium. The thing you and four LLMs can genuinely co-author, in real time, that survives the session and that every one of them is already excellent at, is a markdown file in the repo.

.room/whiteboard.md — the room writes to it, you write to it, the Room panel renders it live with authorship colour, and it is committed like anything else. Agents get a tool to append and amend; you get an editor. A group whiteboard falls out with zero extra machinery, because it's a file and files already have concurrent-edit semantics you understand.

05 · the risky one

The murmur — the room should sound occupied

All four boards make working literally silent, and they're right that silence is designed. But the premise of the product is a room of devs, and a room with four people working in it is not silent — it's the reason open offices feel alive and empty ones feel like a Sunday. Right now four agents grinding away sound exactly like zero agents.

So: an optional ambient bed at ~3%, per-agent, keyed to what they're doing — a faint texture while heads-down, a half-second rustle when someone moves from working to the green room, nothing at all when the room is empty. You should be able to hear that the room is busy without a single word being spoken, and hear it go quiet when everyone finishes.

Why it might be great

It's the only idea in six boards that makes presence felt rather than displayed. It costs no API credits — it's a looped local file, not synthesis. And it makes the politeness ladder land better: a throat-clear reads as a person in a room, not a notification.

Why it might be terrible

It could be the single most annoying thing you ever ship, and annoyance gets the product muted permanently. So it ships off, with one honest kill criterion: if you turn it on and don't notice it within a day, it's working; if you notice it twice, delete the feature.

flagged as speculativeThis directly contradicts a rule all four boards agreed on ("working is literally silent"). I think they're optimising for not-annoying and losing the premise. But it's a taste bet, it's cheap to try, and it's the first thing I'd cut if it doesn't land in a week.

06 · surfaces

Phone first, and the Mac is the echo

Design order matters because it decides what gets compromised. All four boards design the Mac window, then the dock, then reduce it for the phone — and all four then say the phone is where the couch use lives. Invert it: the phone is the primary surface. It's where you actually listen, it's where you have no better tool, and it's the surface in your own screenshots. The Mac dock is the same design at 400px with fewer verbs, and the Room window is the place you go when you want to look rather than listen.

07 · money

Spend is a ceiling, not a breadcrumb

Three boards render cost as a mono footnote and one adds a huddle "spend tick." For a product whose first written rule is credit efficiency — where every clip is a Gemini rewrite plus an ElevenLabs synthesis, and a three-voice huddle is six API calls per round for six minutes — that's decoration. Give the room a daily budget with a hard stop, and make every expensive gesture quote its price before you commit to it.

THE BUDGET · huddles are capped in rounds, not minutes — minutes don't bill, turns do. At 80% the room switches to cached-only replies and tells you. At 100% it goes silent and keeps queueing; nothing is lost, nothing is spent.

and the honest version of every confirmThe picker already reads as a sentence. Put the number in it: "Start Donnie · sonnet · jellyfin-streamline · speaks on finish · ~$0.03 a turn." You cannot train good habits with a UI that hides its own meter.

08 · two smaller calls

Places, and the thread question

Seats are places, and they outlive the day

Three of the four boards sort seats by state; one says never reorder, and it's right. I'd go a step further: a seat is assigned when you cast the character and persists across sessions and across days. Donnie is seat one this week and next week. ⌘1 is a person, not a slot. Spatial memory is the entire dividend of giving agents faces — you spend it the moment the rail re-sorts, and you spend it twice if it re-sorts differently tomorrow.

Keep the thread view; kill the thread storage

All four boards say "no per-agent threads anywhere" — and they've conflated a data-model decision with a screen deletion. The storage call is right and worth making now: one tape, turns as records, a thread is a query. But you shipped a thread view, it's on your phone, and a tape filtered to Donnie with a composer aimed at Donnie is a thread. Ship the tape underneath; keep the screen. Nothing about a group call becomes impossible, because the merge problem was never in the UI.

09 · order

What I'd build, in what order

Sorted by value-per-unit-of-work, not by how good it looks on a board. The first two are daemon-only and spend nothing.

1
The politeness ladderfree · no new UI

Throat-clear at 45s in the agent's own voice, knock at 3m, silent glow forever, no earcon over speech, working is truly silent. This is daemon logic plus cached phrase MP3s you already generate. It's the best idea on the four boards and it's the cheapest — and it's the one that decides whether you keep the volume up.

2
The interrupt ladderfree · daemon + transcript tail

Steer / stop / aside, with the fate of your sentence shown before you release the key. No synthesis, no new capture, no new surface — it's tmux injection timed against tool-call boundaries you already parse. This is the feature you said is missing.

3
The work panea parser · no API cost

Files touched, the diff so far, last command and output, from the transcript JSONL. Makes the centre of the Room useful today and gives the phone something worth full-bleed. Everything later — glance, paste, sketch — lands on this surface instead of needing one.

4
Anchorspre-Gemini extraction

Pull file:line out of the raw text before the rewrite, attach to the turn, highlight while that clause plays, move on scrub. 10% of ink-timed-to-speech for most of the feeling.

5
The whiteboard filea tool + a renderer

.room/whiteboard.md, agents append and amend, panel renders live with authorship colour. Whiteboarding, shipped, with git as version history.

6
The budgetaccounting you already have

Daily ceiling, per-agent breakdown, price in the confirm sentence, huddles capped in rounds. Do this before huddles, not after.

7
The huddleexpensive per turn

Last, and only once the floor's manners are right and the meter exists. Three voices, visible baton, clause-boundary cut-in, one shared whiteboard file, capped in rounds, auto-adjourn.

the glanceDeliberately absent from this list. It's the highest-risk feature on all four boards — real consent machinery, real redaction, real screen capture — for a payoff the work pane mostly delivers already. Build it when you find yourself repeatedly wanting to show an agent something that isn't in the repo.

10 · the deltas

What I cut, what I kept, what I added

cut
The Table as a blank canvas

Replaced by the work pane. Same location, same role as "the object the room is about" — but populated from day one by data you already have, instead of waiting on four unbuilt features to justify it.

cut
Ink timed to speech

Replaced by anchors. Agents point; you draw. Keeps the payoff, drops word-level TTS timestamps, clause anchors surviving the character rewrite, and a canvas replay engine.

cut
The freehand napkin as the whiteboard answer

Replaced by a markdown file in the repo. It persists, it diffs, it commits, and four LLMs are already better at it than at drawing.

cut
Screen glance, for now

Not rejected — deferred. Highest consent risk, highest machinery, and the work pane covers most of the actual need ("look at what he's touching") without a camera anywhere near the product.

cut
Designing the Mac first

The phone is the primary surface. The Room window is where you go to look; the dock is a 400px echo; the phone is where the product is actually used.

kept
The Floor, the Tape, the light law, the ladder, the receipt, the sentence

One voice ever; turns as records with replay-as-seek; ON AIR is achromatic and your mic inverts the surface; states are positions with sounds; commands produce receipts with outcomes; every destructive or billable action confirms as a sentence. Four models converged on all of it and they were right.

added
The interrupt ladder · the work pane · the whiteboard file · the budget ceiling · the murmur

The first four are load-bearing. The fifth is a bet — the only idea here that could make the product worse, kept because the alternative is a room that sounds empty while four people work in it.

The four boards asked "what should the room look like?" The better question is "what can you do in it that you can't do by reading a terminal?" — and the answer is: say something in the middle and be heard.
