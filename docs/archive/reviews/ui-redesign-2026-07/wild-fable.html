Concept board · Room of Devs · one developer, four voices

LAIR FM
the room is a radio station.

Every design instinct says “dashboard.” Resist it. The developer hears this product; the screen only answers three questions: who is talking, what did I miss, and who is waiting on me. So the core artifact isn’t a status grid — it’s the Tape: a scrubbable broadcast of everything the room has said. Every surface is just a different distance from the same speaker: the Door is standing in the hallway, the Studio is walking in, the phone is a walkie-talkie clipped to your belt.

Three consequences fall out of the radio framing, and they drive everything below: (1) missed audio must be recoverable, so the timeline is the hero, not agent cards; (2) interruptions are governed by room etiquette — agents raise hands instead of shouting; (3) talking back is push-to-talk with a visible “errand slip” when you give the room an order, so voice commands stop feeling like a slot machine.

01 / TOKENS

One green for the room. One hue per voice. Amber means “waiting.”

Color is the audio legend. A character’s hue follows their voice across every surface — their avatar, their tape blocks, their speaking ring — so a half-second glance decodes “blue block = Leo said something.” Ooze green is reserved for the room itself (live state, catch-up, PTT). Amber is reserved for things waiting on you. Nothing else may use either.

Two voices of type, matching the product’s two registers: the human register (what characters say — warm ink, generous weight) and the machine register (timestamps, durations, states — always mono, always faint). If it could be spoken aloud it’s set in sans; if it never would be, mono.

02 / SURFACE — THE DOOR

A door left ajar, not a mini dashboard.

The always-on-top sliver shows exactly one thing at a time: whoever holds the floor, mouth moving, with their last clause ticking past. When the room is quiet it collapses to the only fact that matters — who’s waiting for you. It never takes focus; the global hold-key (⌥Space) talks to whoever’s pinned, and a scroll gesture over the Door scrubs the Tape back a few seconds — “wait, what did he just say?” without leaving your editor.

Why one speaker, not four dots? Peripheral vision is great at motion and terrible at reading. A single moving mouth + a colored ring is legible from the corner of your eye; four tiny status LEDs are not. The Door trades completeness for glanceability — the Studio exists precisely so the Door doesn’t have to be complete.
03 / SURFACE — THE STUDIO (main window)

Stage on top, Tape below, Hands on the side.

The main window is the room you walk into when the Door isn’t enough. Top: the cast at their desks — posture, not text, carries state (talking ring, hand raised, dimmed = heads-down working). Bottom: the Tape — every utterance as a colored block you can scrub, with the striped region marking what you haven’t heard. Right: the hand queue, oldest first, each card carrying the actual decision so you can answer without a round trip.

aThe Tape is the hero

Audio evaporates; the Tape makes it durable. Blocks are utterances, hue is speaker, the striped zone is your “away” debt, and Catch up at 2× pays it down in one tap. Scrubbing IS the replay feature — no separate history view to maintain or learn.

bHands, not notifications

“Finished and waiting politely” and “blocked on a decision” are social states, so they look social: a raised hand on the avatar, a queue ordered by patience, and the decision inlined on the card. Amber waits; red pulses. Nothing badges, bounces, or toasts.

cErrand slips make voice trustworthy

A spoken command becomes a visible plan: what the room heard, verbatim, and each step checking off. Misheard you? The slip shows it before anything runs long. This one element is what upgrades voice control from party trick to daily tool.

04 / SURFACE — THE WALKIE-TALKIE (phone)

The phone is not a small Studio. It’s the belt clip.

Couch context: one thumb, half attention, audio through the phone speaker. So the phone gets exactly three zones, stacked by reach — now playing up top (glance), hands in the middle (one tap each), and the bottom third belongs entirely to the thumb: mini-Tape scrubber and a giant hold-to-talk bar. Swipe left/right anywhere on the talk bar to retune which character you’re keyed to; hold with no one tuned and you’re addressing the whole room.

aReach dictates layout

Everything you must touch lives in the bottom 40%; everything you merely read floats above it. The talk bar is fixed and huge because it’s the whole reason the phone surface exists — replying from the couch without sitting up.

bTuning, not navigation

Choosing who to address is a swipe across station chips, like tuning a dial — no per-agent screens to drill into. The keyed-up state takes the whole screen so a pocket press can’t silently broadcast to the room.

cSame Tape everywhere

The phone scrubber and the Studio Tape are the same object at different widths. Pause on the Mac, scrub on the phone, resume from the Door — one playhead, three distances.

05 / ROOM ETIQUETTE & SOUND

Floor rules instead of mute buttons.

Because audio is primary, “notification settings” become house rules — one dial with three positions, visible in the Door and the title bar, switchable by voice (“close the floor”). Sound is designed like radio: each character keeps a two-note entrance sting in their own instrument, so you learn who’s about to speak before a word lands.

06 / WHY THIS DIRECTION

What the radio framing buys that a dashboard can’t.

1It designs for the miss

The honest failure mode of an audio tool is “I wasn’t listening.” Dashboards ignore that; the Tape is built around it. Away-time is drawn as debt, catch-up is one tap, and replay-by-scrubbing needs zero new concepts.

2Characters get manners, not just voices

The playfulness lives in behavior — stings, raised hands, holding the floor — which stays charming at hour six, where costume-heavy UI wears out. The avatars are small; the personality is temporal.

3One mental model, three distances

Door, Studio, walkie-talkie are the same station heard from the hallway, the chair, and the couch. Learn the Tape and the hand queue once; every surface is a crop of them. That’s also the implementation story: shared components, different viewports.

4Voice control you can watch

Errand slips give spoken plans a body: verbatim transcript, steps, checkmarks. Trust in voice UIs dies from silent misfires; the slip makes every misfire visible in under a second, and every success quietly satisfying.
