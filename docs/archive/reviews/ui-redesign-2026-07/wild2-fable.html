Room of Devs · Concept board · Round 2

ONE ROOM, ONE TAPE.

The room is a place, and everything that happens in it — every spoken line, sketch, screen-glance, decision, and reply — lands as a moment on one shared tape. There are no per-agent threads to hunt through. You experience the same room at three distances: overheard (a tiny strip over your other apps), at the table (the Mac window), and pocketed (the phone). Sound tells you that something happened and how it feels; the tape is there when your ears need receipts.

01Thesis: three distances, one room

Every surface is the same room seen from a different distance — never a different app with its own mental model. Anything you can hear, you can scrub back to. Anything an agent shows you (a sketch, a glance at your screen) is pinned to the moment it was said, so audio and imagery never drift apart.

Distance 1 · Overheard

The Strip

A pill of faces floating over whatever you're doing. It never takes focus. It answers one question at a glance: who's talking, who's waiting, is anything burning? Hold a face to talk. Drop a file on a face to hand it over.

Distance 2 · At the table

The Table

The main window. Seats on the left, a shared paper napkin in the middle where anyone — agent or you — can draw, decisions parked under the lamp, and the tape running along the bottom.

Distance 3 · Pocketed

The Earpiece

The phone is not a mini dashboard — it's the room in your pocket. Listen live, thumb the talk bar, settle decisions with one tap, sketch back with your thumb, spawn a new dev from the couch.

02Tokens

A dim studio at night, lit by lamps. Character colors are for identity; the four lamp colors are for state — and each lamp color has a matching earcon, so the palette is audible before it's visible. Neutral surfaces stay desaturated so a single lit lamp reads across the room.

05Screen glance — turning the laptop toward a coworker

Screen awareness is a gesture with a lamp, not a setting. You hold a face and say "look at this" (or press ⌥G on a face). The violet frame lights around your actual screen for the duration — the same violet everywhere it's mentioned — with a shutter tick at start and end. The glance becomes a tape moment with a thumbnail, so "what exactly did Donnie see?" always has an answer.

Consent grammar: glances are always started by you, always framed, always time-boxed (15s default; hold longer to extend), and always logged on the tape. No frame, no glance — the frame IS the permission indicator, not a notification about one.

06Table talk — the group call

When a problem needs more than one head, you pull chairs to the middle: "Leo, Donnie, table talk about the retry queue." The room's audio ducks to those voices; characters can interject and build on each other because they share one live transcript and the napkin. The call is a single stretch of tape, not three parallel threads pretending to be a meeting.

07The Earpiece — the phone

One hand, three jobs: know (is anything under the lamp?), answer (talk bar and big decision buttons live in the thumb zone), send back (voice, text, a photo, or a thumb-drawn scribble). Everything above the thumb zone is glanceable; everything actionable is below it.

08Rationale — the five bets

1 · One tape beats N threads

An audio-first tool lives or dies on "wait, what did he say?" A single time-ordered tape makes replay, voice commands ("replay Donnie's last message"), sketches, and glances all the same operation: seek. Per-agent history still exists — it's just a filter on the tape, never a separate place.

2 · State is a lamp with a sound

Four states, four colors, four earcons, everywhere identical. The crucial asymmetry: working is silent, waiting knocks once, only blocked repeats. A room that respects your attention this precisely is one you'll keep the volume up on.

3 · The napkin makes drawing structural

Drawing isn't an attachment type; it's the room's shared table-top, present in the main window's center and in every reply surface. Agents sketch while speaking, you circle while talking back — picture and voice are one moment, which is how explaining actually works.

4 · Consent you can see from across the room

The violet frame is the glance. No frame, no access — and every glance leaves a thumbnail on the tape. That's what lets "look at this error" feel as casual as tilting a laptop: the trust is ambient, not buried in a settings pane.

5 · Distances, not features, define the surfaces

Strip, Table, and Earpiece don't get their own concepts — the same seats, lamps, napkin, and tape appear at every distance, scaled to attention available. Typed replies, attachments, and the group call therefore exist everywhere by construction, not by porting.
