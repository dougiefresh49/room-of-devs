Room of Devs · open concept pass · one-developer tool

The room is a table,
not a timeline.

Every version of this product wants to be a chat app: cards, threads, scrollback, one lane per agent. But the developer never reads it — they hear it. So the screen shouldn't show what was said. It should show what is being talked about: one shared surface in the middle of the room where the screenshot, the sketch, the error, the pasted file lands, and the characters gather around it. Voice carries the conversation; the table carries the object. Screen-sharing, drawing, group calls and attachments stop being four features — they are all things you put on the table.

Audio-first · UI is the companion 3 surfaces: Table · Shoulder · Pocket One input, everywhere Floor control > thread routing
01

The model

Three ideas do all the structural work. Everything else in this board falls out of them.

Idea one

The Table

One shared canvas the whole room can see and mark up. A screenshot you tossed in, a napkin Donnie is sketching, a stack trace, a pasted PNG from your phone. There is exactly one table, and it holds exactly one thing at a time — with a shelf of what was on it before. The table is the only place attachments, drawings and screen glances live, which is why those features need no new UI.

Idea two

The Floor

Only one voice speaks at a time — that's not a limitation, it's the interface. Whoever holds the floor is lit; everyone else is dimmed and queued with a one-line preview of what they want to say. You can hand the floor, skip it, or take it back by holding the talk key. Group calls are just the floor being passed between more than two people. Turn-taking is the room's core mechanic, and it's visible at every size.

Idea three

Posture, not badges

An agent's state is expressed the way a person in a room expresses it. Working = turned away, dim. Speaking = lit and leaning in, mouth frames swapping. Waiting politely = turned toward you, hand up, holding still. Blocked = stood up, hatched red, can't be ignored. Four postures, readable at 28px on a phone and at 16px in the menu-bar strip.

Mac · always visible, never focused

Voice command, visible

A room command isn't magic — the capsule shows the plan as it executes: pause ✓ → find Donnie's last → replay ▸ with a two-second undo. If the room misheard, you see it before it happens.

Three sound modes

Out loud (speakers) · Headphones (same audio, room mic off, typed replies preferred) · Silent (nobody speaks; the capsule becomes a reading strip and the say-line is the transcript). Same UI, three volumes of the same room.

Why one capsule, not a menu-bar icon

A menu-bar icon can show a count but not a sentence. The whole value of the always-on surface is reading the half-line that's being spoken without switching apps — that needs ~40 characters of width, not 16 pixels.

06

The Napkin — drawing that is timed to speech

Agents don't render finished diagrams. They draw while they talk: each stroke lands on the beat of the sentence that explains it, so the picture and the voice are one thing. Scrub the audio and the ink rewinds with it. This is the feature that makes audio-first pay off — the drawing is the visual channel of the same utterance.

07

The Huddle — one conversation, several voices

Drag two or more seats to the front of the table and they're in a huddle: one shared channel, one shared transcript, one table. Agents hear each other because they're literally in the same conversation context — so Donnie can say “Leo's right about the null, but…”. What makes it feel like a call rather than a queue is the floor: a visible baton with a queue and a predicted next speaker, that you can seize at any moment by holding the key.

The rule that keeps it sane

Max three voices in a huddle. Four AI characters talking to each other is comedy, not work. The fourth seat is always yours.

Cutting in

Holding the key ducks the current speaker to 20% within 120ms and stops them at the next clause boundary — not mid-word. The room yields to you the way polite people do.

Cost is visible, because it's real

A huddle is the most expensive thing here (every turn is a synthesis). The floor beam carries a faint spend tick, and huddles auto-adjourn after 2 minutes of your silence.

08

Surface three — The Pocket

Phone · one thumb

The phone is not a smaller Table. On the couch you're not marking up screenshots — you're on a call with the room. So the phone opens straight into a call-shaped screen: who's talking, what's waiting, a giant talk button. Everything interactive sits in the bottom 41%. The Table's canvas appears here only when there's something on it — and then it's full-bleed, because that's the one thing worth looking at.

What the phone is FOR

Three verbs, nothing else: listen, unblock, dispatch. No settings, no logs, no roster management. If it needs two hands or a keyboard, it belongs on the Table.

Attachments are first-class here

Photo, screenshot, file — the phone's Photo button is a shortcut to the Table. Whatever you send from the couch is on the Mac's table when you sit back down, on the shelf.

Silent phone, loud room

Audio routing is one toggle: the room speaks here or there, never both. The device not holding the audio still shows the karaoke line, so you can follow along muted in a meeting.

09

The Ledger — the transcript is a drawer, not a home

There is scrollback; it just isn't the product. One drawer, one stream, every voice in it — because the room had one conversation, not six. Pull it open when you missed something, push it shut. Objects that were on the table appear inline as thumbnails, and every line is replayable for free.

10

Why this, and what I cut

The four "near future" features are one feature

Screen glances, drawings, attachments and pastes are all an object entering the conversation. Give the room one place for objects and all four ship as the same surface with different sources. That's the whole reason for the Table.

Group calls demand a shared channel from day one

You cannot bolt a multi-voice call onto per-agent threads — you'd have to invent a merge. So this concept has no per-agent thread at all: one room, one ledger, and "talking to Leo alone" is just a huddle of one. The expensive architectural decision is made up front.

Audio-first means the UI shows the present tense

Chat UIs show the past. A room shows now: who has the floor, the sentence in flight, the thing being pointed at. That's why the Table has no scroll and the karaoke line exists — catching up mid-sentence is the actual daily task.

Posture beats badges for glanceability

At capsule size you have ~16px of avatar and half a sentence. Dim/lit/ringed/hatched survives that; icon+color+count doesn't. And it keeps identity hue sacred, which matters when one of your characters is literally named after the color red.

Consent is a gesture, not a setting

Screen awareness is the feature most likely to feel creepy in a tool that runs all day. Making it a physical toss with a visible countdown, a redaction brush, and a 5-minute expiry means the developer never wonders what the room can see.

The phone earns its own shape

A responsive Table on a phone would be unusable one-handed. Recasting the phone as a call with three verbs makes it genuinely better than the Mac at the couch job — and the Table's canvas still arrives full-bleed when there's something to see.

Deliberately cut

✕
Per-agent chat threads and tabs
The single biggest source of the "it's just Slack with turtles" feeling, and structurally incompatible with the group call.
✕
A dashboard of agent stats
Tokens, model names, runtimes: one line in the seat rail, never a panel. This tool is for hearing, not for monitoring.
✕
Notification badges and unread counts
Replaced by hands-up posture, which decays into the pile-up. Nothing here accumulates guilt.
✕
More than three voices in a huddle
Both a taste call and a cost call — every extra voice is another synthesis per turn.
✕
Persistent screen sharing
One frame, five minutes, then gone. A glance, never a camera.
