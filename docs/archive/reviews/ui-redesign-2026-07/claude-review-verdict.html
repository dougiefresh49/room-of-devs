Room of Devs · review of four final synthesis boards

Four boards.
One design.

Floor & Table, The Workbench, One Floor/One Table/One Tape, and The Den are not four concepts — they are one concept at four levels of detail. All four land on the same trinity, the same achromatic ON AIR, the same luminance inversion for talkback, the same "no per-agent threads," the same three-voice huddle, the same confirm-as-a-sentence picker. That convergence is real evidence and you should bank it. But it also means the interesting question isn't which board wins — it's the eight places they actually disagree, and the five things none of them designed.

01 · the convergence

Settled. Stop re-litigating these.

Every one of these appears in all four boards, arrived at independently. Treat them as decided and spend your design budget elsewhere.

Floor — one voice, ever Tape — one time-ordered record; replay is seek Table — objects enter the conversation in one place ON AIR is achromatic — luminance, never hue Talkback inverts — output = light on; input = becomes light lamp + a mono word, never color alone persona hue = jersey only (avatar, spine, meter, ink) dock = 4 earned states, no scroll "Play it / Later" two-button decision dock phone = 3 verbs, bottom 40% huddle = 3 voices max, clause-boundary cut-in, auto-adjourn interpreter = a receipt, not a toast picker confirms as a sentence, persona first kill-confirm names what you lose avatar = bare <img>, siblings do the chrome

The strongest signal in the whole program: four independent models, given the same brief, all decided the main window should stop being a grid of agent cards.

02 · board by board

What works, what doesn't

Floor & Table

grok-4.5 · 2,300 words
reference · thinnest
  • The clearest statement of the idea. "Three objects; every surface is a different distance from them" is the one-sentence version of what the other three take 7,000 words to say. If you ever pitch this tool, pitch it in these words.
  • "Three distances" (Strip / Den / Earpiece) is a better mental model than the others' "three surfaces" — distance implies the same content at different resolution, which is exactly right.
  • Honest about its own rejections: naming what it threw away and why is why the board is trustworthy.
  • It's a summary, not a design. Almost every hard part is named and not solved — the glance is a screenshot of a screenshot, the huddle floor is a row of pills, the interpreter receipt has three rows and no destructive gate.
  • Keeps violet for glance consent while Donatello is purple. Two boards caught this collision; this one didn't.
  • The Strip is input-free "and that's the design" — but typed reply is called structural three sections earlier. The contradiction is unresolved.
keep"Same room at three distances," the table shelf (one object live, the rest on a shelf), and huddle auto-adjourn. Read it first, build from another.

The Workbench

gpt-5.6 Sol · 2,100 words
two ideas worth stealing
  • BET 03 — the cast rail never reorders. This is the single most contrarian call in the program and I think it's correct, against the other three. Stable position is the entire payoff of giving agents faces; a rail that re-sorts on state change throws away the spatial memory you paid for. Hot seats should expand in place.
  • The consent stack is the most complete. Preflight redaction editing, a named audience, pixel expiry separate from record expiry, and a durable metadata receipt — "inspectable before, during, and after." The others have three of those four at best.
  • "Audio etiquette is product design" and "a lovable room is one you do not mute" — the right frame, stated more plainly than anywhere else.
  • Thinnest treatment of the tape — it's a sidebar list, not a scrubbable object, which quietly loses the replay/catch-up/history unification the other boards get for free.
  • Hand-up in amber collides with its own hazard usage and with Mikey.
  • The workbench/room/shoulder/pocket naming is generic where the others' is specific. Minor, but naming is most of what a concept board does.
keepThe stable rail (over three boards' state-sorted ladder) and the full four-stage consent model. Discard the rest.

One Floor, One Table, One Tape

opus5 · 7,200 words
strongest · build from this
  • "Six places to stand" + the politeness ladder. States as positions in a room with a location, a lamp, a posture and a sound, plus a concrete escalation (45s throat-clear in their own voice → 3m knock → silent glow forever, never twice at the same volume). This is the best idea in the entire program and it is also nearly free to build — it's daemon logic plus cached phrase MP3s you already generate.
  • The baton as one object — speak, type, attach, show, all through a single component rendered at three sizes. This is what actually makes "typed replies and attachments everywhere" structural instead of a feature request.
  • It does the engineering. Type-through on a non-activating panel is spelled out as a contract (global key monitor, self-drawn caret, Esc releases, named fallback) instead of waved at. Kill-confirm names the cost. Scroll-the-dock-to-scrub is a real gesture nobody else found.
  • Only board with a build order, and it's the right one: floor + queue + ladder alone for a week, and if that feels like directing rather than falling behind, everything else follows.
  • "Amber is you" — a third color system is one too many, and the board admits the problem in the same paragraph ("Mikey's orange lives nearby"). Amber is also its hazard color and its skip-permissions flag.
  • Longest and densest; the sheer volume makes it hard to tell load-bearing from decorative.
keepNearly all of it. The state model, the ladder, the sound tokens, the baton, the tape-as-turns, the ears-only phone screen, the build order.

The Den

fable-5 · 5,300 words
strongest on taste · nearly a twin
  • The best-looking room. Warm aubergine over blue-black, "a den at 1am, not a terminal theme" — and it's the palette closest to what your product already looks like on the phone.
  • "Your ink is light." One law instead of two: everything that comes from you is luminance — your mic, your ink, your circle. This beats a third color system and it costs nothing.
  • Glance-green as a reserved consent color, freeing violet for Donnie and amber for hazard. Correct.
  • The only board that designed call mode — the thing you actually shipped. Caption fade-stack (newest bright, older fading, so you can rejoin a call you half-heard) and ghost tape moments for non-billing intermediates.
  • Agent-initiated glance requests as a dock state: "asking is their move, showing is always yours."
  • Rejects dock type-through hard and routes typing to the Room composer — defensible, but it hand-waves the cost (you just lost focus, which is the whole thing the dock exists to protect).
  • Adopts everything from prior rounds so faithfully that its own additions are hard to find. The provenance rigor is admirable and it slightly crowds out invention.
keepThe palette, "your ink is light," glance-green, call mode with the caption fade-stack, and the agent-initiated glance ask.
03 · the forks

Eight real disagreements, decided

These are the only places the four boards actually diverge. Everything else is phrasing. My call on each, with the reason — this table is the reused-parts design.

ForkThe splitPickWhy
Seat order State-sorted density ladder (F&T, 1F1T1T, Den) vs. stable rail that never reorders (Workbench) Stable rail Faces exist so you can find someone without reading. Re-sorting deletes that. Three boards hedge with "freeze the top slot while the pointer's inside," which protects the click and not the mental map. Let hot seats expand, never move.
Your color "Amber is you" as a third system (1F1T1T) vs. "your ink is light" (Den) vs. nothing (F&T, Workbench) Your ink is light One law — everything from you is luminance — instead of two systems that fight. Amber stays hazard-only, which is the only way it survives next to Mikey.

Dock typing Type-through via global key monitor (1F1T1T) vs. no inputs, ever (Den, F&T, Workbench) No inputs — with the fallback promoted The dock's job is not stealing focus; a self-drawn caret over a captured global key monitor is a lot of fragility for one field. Take opus5's fallback as the design: ⌥⏎ summons the Room with the baton already armed and aimed. One keystroke, no NSPanel heroics.

04 · the gaps

Five things none of the four designed

This is where I'd spend the next round. Each of these is either the thing you actually said is missing, or a place where all four boards specified something they can't afford.

GAP 01

Interruption is designed at the wrong layer

You said the live call is "mostly just the agent speaking and I listen." The fix isn't a talk button — you have one. It's what happens downstream of it. All four boards define cutting in as an audio operation: duck to 20% in 120ms, stop at a clause boundary. That's TTS. The actual question is whether your sentence reaches the agent before it finishes the turn — and whether it lands as a course correction, a stop, or a note it reads at the end. Those are three completely different outcomes and the UI must never confuse them. Zero of four boards mention it.

GAP 02

The Table is empty on day one

All four put a shared canvas in the middle of the main window and justify it as "the four futures fall out of it." Fine — but glance, napkin, huddle and paste are all unbuilt. Ship any of these boards tomorrow and the center of your primary surface holds the last spoken summary and a shelf with nothing on it. Not one board says what the Table shows before the futures arrive, which is a real hole in the only layout decision that matters.

GAP 03

Ink timed to speech is the most expensive idea in the program and nobody priced it

"Each stroke lands on the beat of the clause that explains it, and rewinds when you scrub" requires: the model emitting a structured stroke list with clause anchors, Gemini preserving those anchors through the character rewrite, ElevenLabs word-level timestamps (a different endpoint than the stream you use), and a canvas replay engine bound to the audio clock. It is presented as a motion-budget decision. It is a subsystem — and it's the single item least likely to survive contact with real model output.

GAP 04

Money is decoration

Three boards show spend as a mono breadcrumb and one shows a huddle "spend tick." But a three-voice huddle is three Gemini rewrites plus three ElevenLabs syntheses per round, and the boards let it run six minutes. For a product whose first documented rule is credit efficiency, cost should be a ceiling, not a garnish.

GAP 05

The Mac gets designed first and the phone gets designed third

Every board orders it Room → dock → Pocket, and every board then admits the phone is where the couch use happens. Your own screenshots are all phone. The Mac already has a perfectly good UI for watching an agent work: the terminal. The phone is the only surface where this tool has no competition.
