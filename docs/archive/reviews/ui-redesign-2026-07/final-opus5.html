Room of Devs · final synthesis concept · dark-first

One floor.
One table.
One tape.

Seven agents can work in parallel; they cannot speak in parallel. So the product isn't a wall of cards — it's a room with three pieces of furniture. The floor is who has the mic. The table is the one thing the room is currently looking at. The tape is everything that was said, drawn, shown and decided, in one time-ordered stream. Screen glances, sketches, attachments, typed replies and the group call are not four features bolted on — they are things that happen on that furniture.

FURNITURE 01

The Floor

Exactly one voice at a time. Who holds it, who's queued, who's blocked. A group call is just the floor being passed between more than two — and you can seize it at any moment by holding the baton.

FURNITURE 02

The Table

One shared surface holding one object: a screen glance, a napkin sketch, a pasted stack trace, a decision under the lamp. Agents draw on it; you draw back. Zero new UI for four "future" features.

FURNITURE 03

The Tape

Every turn lands as one moment — voice + ink + object + outcome bundled together. Replay is seek. Scrub back and the drawing rewinds in sync with the sentence that explained it. No per-agent threads, anywhere.

PROVENANCE Floor/queue/green-room comes from the open round's "four places to stand"; the Table is the round-2 board that noticed glance + sketch + paste are one idea; the Tape is the radio-station board's scrubbable broadcast. Fusing all three is the move: prior boards each had two of the three and had to bolt the missing one on.

02 / The state model

Six places to stand — and a politeness ladder

State isn't a badge on a card; it's a position in a room, and every position has a location on screen, a lamp, a posture frame and a sound. An agent is always in exactly one place. Everything else in this board is a consequence of this table.

The politeness ladder is the feature

An agent that finishes while you're deep in something doesn't interrupt — it walks to the green room. At 45s it clears its throat (200ms, its own voice, 20% volume). At 3 min, one soft knock. After 6 min it goes quiet and just glows, forever. It never asks twice at the same volume. That is what "waits politely" actually means, and it's an audio design, not a UI one.

Three room volumes, one room

Out loud — speakers, full cast. Headphones — same audio, room mic prefers typed replies. Silent — nobody speaks; the Sill becomes a reading strip and the tape's say-line is the transcript. Every feature in this board works in all three; silent mode is why typed replies are structural rather than a fallback.

Sound tokens

The unusual half of the system. All under 400ms, in the room's own key, duckable independently of voices. Rule: no earcon ever plays over a voice — they queue behind speech and collapse (three throat-clears become one).

The consent stance

The room can never look on its own. There is no “always allow.” An agent that wants to see says so out loud — “can I see what you're looking at?” — and the Sill sprouts a single Show him button that expires in 20 seconds. Asking is the agent's move; showing is always yours. The amber frame around your screen is the permission indicator, so you can never wonder what the room can see.

The napkin — ink that is timed to speech

Agents don't render finished diagrams and drop them on you. They draw while they talk: each stroke lands on the beat of the clause that explains it, so the picture and the voice are one object. Scrub the tape and the ink rewinds with the audio. This is the thing that makes an audio-first tool pay off — the drawing is the visual channel of the same utterance.

06 / The baton

One object for talking back — voice, text, and everything you can paste

Addressing is physical, not navigational. You don't open Donnie's thread; you take the baton and aim it at Donnie. It exists at three sizes — a bar in the Room, a bar on the Sill, a round key on the phone — with one muscle memory: hold to arm (~600ms fill, so there are no accidental hot mics), speak or type, release to send, drag off to cancel.

Four ways to use the same object

🎙 SpeakPrimary. Room ducks to 20% within 120ms and stops the current speaker at the next clause boundary, not mid-word.
⌨ TypeStart typing instead of holding. Headphones on → hear the reply. Silent mode → pure text, nobody speaks. Same turn, same tape.
📎 Attach⌘V or drag. Image, file, log, screenshot from your phone. It lands on the table, addressed to whoever the baton is aimed at.
◱ ShowWhile holding, "show" attaches a glance to this turn — the picture and the sentence arrive together.

Aim

Hold a seat, press ⌘1…7, or say a name. Hold the baton with nobody aimed at and you're addressing the room — that's how you give a voice command, and how you open a huddle.

→ Donnie→ Leo→ Raph→ the room
swipe across the chips on phone · ⌘1…7 on Mac · "hey Donnie" by voice

The moment you take it, everyone else drops to 45% and the room auto-holds. One mic, one channel — the same rule that governs the floor.

PROVENANCE Hold-a-card-not-a-thread and the 600ms arming fill come from the detailed round; swipe-to-retune from the radio-station board; clause-boundary ducking from round 2. ADDED: unifying speak / type / attach / show into one object rather than a PTT key plus a separate composer. That's what makes "typed replies and attachments everywhere" structural: there is only one reply surface in the product, rendered at three sizes.

07 / The huddle

The group call is the floor being passed between more than two

Say “Leo, Donnie — huddle about the retry queue,” or drag two seats to the front of the table. They share one live transcript and one table, so Donnie can say “Leo's right about the null, but—”. What makes it feel like a call instead of queued monologues is the visible baton: who has it, who wants it next, and the fact that you can seize it mid-sentence.

Three AI characters plus you. Four agents talking to each other is comedy, not work — and it's a taste call and a cost call, since every turn is a synthesis.

08 / The tape

Every turn is one moment: voice + ink + object + outcome

The honest failure mode of an audio tool is “I wasn't listening.” A dashboard ignores that; the tape is built around it. Away-time is drawn as debt, catch-up pays it down in one tap, and replay-by-scrubbing needs zero new concepts. There is scrollback — it just isn't the home screen. Per-agent history exists as a filter on the tape, never as a separate place.

09 / Surface three — the Pocket

The phone is not a smaller Room. It's a call with the room.

On the couch you're not marking up screenshots — you're keeping half an ear on a conversation. So the phone gets three verbs and nothing else: listen · unblock · dispatch. Everything interactive lives in the bottom 41%; the top is read-only by design so a thumb can never fire an action by accident. The table appears here only when there's something on it — and then it's full-bleed, because that's the one thing worth looking at.

  • It shows the interpretation, not just the transcript. “Pause everything” became HOLD THE ROOM with an explicit promise — queue held, nothing dropped. That sentence is the whole trust model.
  • Inferred steps are labelled inferred. The system may be helpful as long as it admits which part it made up and gives you one word to cancel it.
  • Every step is individually undoable. A plan you can only abandon wholesale isn't a plan, it's a gamble.
  • It reads itself aloud only if you're not looking — after 30s of Mac idle. Otherwise it stays silent, because you read faster than it talks.

12 / Rationale

The contested calls, decided

01 · Furniture, not features

Ten boards, and the ones that aged best all replaced "a grid of agent cards" with a spatial metaphor. But each held only part of it: some had the floor and no shared object, some had the table and no durable record. Floor + table + tape is the smallest set that makes every requirement — including all four near-future ones — fall out as a consequence instead of a new screen. Screen glance = an object arriving at the table. Sketch = ink on the table timed to a turn. Attachment = a paste onto the table. Group call = the floor passed among three. Typed reply = the baton, silent.

02 · Light for output, inversion for input

Speaking is achromatic — any hue for the most important state eventually collides with four saturated personas. But every prior board also made push-to-talk white, so "he's talking" and "you're talking" looked alike. The fix is the system's spine: output puts light on the surface; input turns the surface into light. Used nowhere else, readable on the Sill from peripheral vision and on the phone from across a room.

03 · Amber is you

A shared canvas needs an authorship cue that survives at 28px. Giving the developer a reserved color — ink, glance frame, open mic, held room — does that, kills the violet-vs-Donnie collision two boards had, and makes consent legible from across the room: if it's amber, you did it.

04 · No per-agent threads, anywhere

The single biggest source of the "it's just Slack with turtles" feeling, and structurally incompatible with a group call — you'd have to invent a merge. So there is one tape, and "just Donnie" is a filter, never a place. This is the expensive architectural decision, made up front and on purpose.

05 · Silence is designed

The failure mode of an audio-first tool isn't ugliness, it's annoyance — and annoyance gets the product muted permanently in week two. Hence the politeness ladder, the no-earcon-over-speech rule, working being literally silent, and only "blocked" repeating. A room that respects your attention this precisely is one you keep the volume up on.

06 · Cost is visible, quietly

Every clip is a rewrite plus a synthesis. So: cached, no spend under a replay, "these don't bill" over live intermediates, a spend tick on the huddle beam, the model named in the confirm sentence. One mono line each, never a banner. A UI that hides its own cost trains expensive habits.

What I'd cut

  • Per-agent chat threads and tabs. One tape, filters instead. Non-negotiable if the group call is ever going to work.
  • The agent-card grid. Seats carry strictly more meaning per pixel and free the window's middle for the table.
  • A room-level transport bar. Transport belongs to a channel: the floor card, the Sill, the player.
  • Unread badges. The green room replaces them — "unread" implies a debt you owe the tool.
  • Metrics, token counters, cost charts. One mono breadcrumb where money was actually spent; never a panel.
  • Density and grid preferences. Density is set by state. There is one right layout for a given room.

What I'd build first

The floor + the queue + the Sill, with the politeness ladder wired up and nothing else. If a week of that feels like a room you're directing rather than a feed you're behind on, everything else here follows. Then, in order:

  • The baton — voice and typed, aimed, with paste. Everything downstream is a reply surface.
  • The tape — turns as records; replay and catch-up come free.
  • The table — glance first (it's a screenshot on a canvas), then agent ink, then your ink.
  • The huddle last, because it's the most expensive per turn and it needs the floor's manners to already be right.
