Room of Devs — concept board

The room has one microphone. Design the microphone, not the dashboard.

Every version of this tool that starts as a grid of agent cards is designing for a channel it doesn't have. Seven agents can work in parallel; they cannot speak in parallel. Ears are single-lane. So the scarce resource in this product isn't screen space or status — it's the floor: who is talking right now, who is next, and who gets bumped.

So: the app is a soundstage, and the developer is the director. There are exactly four places an agent can stand — on the floor, in the queue, in the green room, or at the door — and moving between them is the whole interaction model. The by-product of a day's work is a script: a timestamped, scrubbable screenplay of everything the room said, which is also the search index, the replay control, and the log.

Two things follow that a card grid never gets to. First, state is expressed in sound before sight — this board ships sound tokens next to the color tokens, because an agent finishing is an earcon, and only secondarily a badge. Second, the phone is a walkie-talkie, not a smaller Mac app — its default state is screen-off, one thumb, earbuds in.

01

Four places to stand

The entire state model. Not badges on cards — positions in a room. Everything else in this board is a consequence of this table. An agent is always in exactly one place, and a place is a location on screen, a color, a posture frame, and a sound.

PlaceMeans What the developer does about itSound
The Floor Speaking now. Exactly one, ever. Listen. Or duck it, skip it, or hold the card and talk back. voice + light hum
The Queue Has something to say, waiting for the mic. Reorder by dragging. Drag to the top = "you, next." silent (by design)
Green Room Finished. Waiting politely for attention. Nothing, safely. It escalates its own volume over time. throat-clear ladder
The Door Blocked. Cannot continue without a decision. Answer it. This is the only thing that can jump the queue. knock (2 taps)
Heads-down Working. Nothing to say. Nothing. Deliberately low-contrast. —
Asleep Idle or session ended. Wake it with a line, or let it go. —

The politeness ladder is the feature. An agent that finishes while you're deep in something doesn't interrupt — it walks to the green room. At 45s it clears its throat (a 200ms breath in its own voice, at 20% volume). At 3 minutes, a soft knock. After 6 minutes it goes fully quiet and just glows, forever. It never asks twice at the same volume. That's what "waits politely" actually means, and it's an audio design, not a UI one.

04

Surface 2 — the Chime Strip

The always-visible, never-focus-stealing Mac surface. It floats above other apps at the bottom of the screen. It is a visual echo of the audio, nothing more — and it is deliberately illegible at a distance except for three facts: who is talking, how many are waiting, and is anyone at the door. You should be able to read it with peripheral vision in under 200ms without moving your hands off the keyboard.

D 2 1

Speaking. The wave IS the lip-sync signal — same data driving the avatar frames. Amber ring = the floor.

R needs a decision Answer

Door state. The only time the strip grows and shows words. It shrinks back the instant you answer. Nothing else can expand it.

CL working

Quiet room. Fades to 55% after 10s of silence and shrinks to a stack of faces. This is what it looks like 80% of the day.

You → Donnie · release to send

You holding ⌘'. The strip is also the mic indicator — it's the only visual confirmation that you're being recorded, and it never takes focus to show it.

Why a strip and not a menu-bar item or a HUD of cards: menu-bar items are a click away from anything and invisible while you're in a full-screen editor. Cards invite reading, which is exactly the wrong instinct for a surface that must not steal attention. A strip that mostly shows a waveform trains you to glance and dismiss — the eye confirms what the ear already told you and goes back to the code. The strip has no scroll, no list, and no way to open anything except the door.

05

Surface 3 — the Cue Sheet

Voice commands to the room become a visible little plan. Not a chat reply, not a toast: a numbered cue sheet that runs in front of you and can be stopped mid-way. It appears above the Chime Strip, runs, and dissolves. The point is that a spoken command to a machine deserves the same accountability as a script — you see what it heard, what it decided that means, and where it is.

  • It shows the interpretation, not just the transcript. "Pause everything" became PAUSE ROOM with an explicit promise — queue held, nothing dropped. That sentence is the whole trust model.
  • Step 3 is inferred and labelled as inferred. The system is allowed to be helpful as long as it admits which part it made up, and gives you one word to cancel it.
  • Every step is undoable individually. A plan that can only be abandoned wholesale isn't a plan, it's a gamble.
  • It is spoken back only if you're not looking. The cue sheet has a "read it to me" fallback that fires automatically when the Mac has been idle 30s+ — i.e. you're across the room. Otherwise it stays silent, because you can read faster than it can talk.
  • Same object on the phone. The cue sheet is the one component that renders identically on both surfaces, because it's the only place where the developer's intent and the room's interpretation have to be compared.
06

Surface 4 — the phone (a walkie-talkie)

The phone is not a smaller Mac app. On the couch you are not supervising a room, you're keeping half an ear on it. So the phone's job is: hear it, say something back, and occasionally start someone new. Everything sits in the bottom third — the top two-thirds is read-only by design, so a thumb can never fire an action by accident.

07

How characters express state

Avatars are frame-swapped images, so posture carries state and badges are the backup. Six postures per character; the mouth frames layer on top only for the one who has the floor. This is the single biggest reason the UI can stay this quiet — a character standing differently is legible at 40px in peripheral vision, and a text badge is not.

Motion budget: the room animates in exactly two places — the mouth of whoever has the floor, and the waveform. Everything else is static. A room where five things twitch is a room you learn to stop looking at, which would kill the glance-and-dismiss contract the Chime Strip depends on.

08

Rationale — and what I deliberately didn't build

Why this shape

  • The channel is one-lane, so the UI is a queue. Any parallel card grid quietly lies about what's happening: five "done" badges glowing at once when only one voice can reach you. Making turn-taking the primary object makes the interface honest and gives the developer the one control they actually want — who talks next.
  • Priority order is a physical order. Door above queue above green room isn't a sort setting; it's how the room is arranged, top to bottom, always. You learn the geometry once and then read it without reading.
  • The script makes the audio addressable. Speech is the worst medium for re-finding things. A transcript that doubles as replay control and search turns a lossy channel into a durable one — and it costs nothing, because the text already exists upstream of the voice.
  • Sound tokens make silence designed rather than accidental. The politeness ladder, the "no earcon over speech" rule, and duck-vs-pause exist because the failure mode of an audio-first tool isn't ugliness, it's annoyance — and annoyance gets the whole product muted, permanently, in week two.
  • Playfulness lives in behavior, not decoration. The characters are charming because Raph blocks the door and Mikey gets ducked — not because of gradients or confetti. A tool you stare at all day earns its personality through timing and voice, and pays for it with a restrained surface.

Deliberately absent

No agent card grid

The default answer, and the one that fights the audio channel hardest. Lanes replace it and carry strictly more meaning per pixel.

No notification-center toasts

Sound is the notification. A toast for something you just heard is a second interruption for one event, and it steals a glance the strip already gave you.

No metrics, tokens, or cost charts

Nothing here counts anything. This surface is for hearing the room; a number that only ever goes up will otherwise colonize the whole layout.

No unread badges

The green room replaces them. "Unread" implies a debt you owe the tool; the green room implies someone patient who'll still be there later.

No chat composer on the Mac

Typing to an agent is what the agent's own terminal is for. The room is for talking, and holding one key is faster than any text box.

The one thing to build first, to know if this is right: the floor + the queue + the Chime Strip, with the politeness ladder wired up and nothing else. If a week of that feels like a room you're directing rather than a feed you're behind on, everything else on this board follows. If it doesn't, no amount of script, casting, or cue sheet will save it.
