Room of Devs · synthesis · dark-first · locked-target candidate

Talkback One room, one voice at a time. The interface's whole job is to answer who's talking, what did they say, and who's waiting on me — before you've finished turning your head.

Thesis. Sound has a direction, so light does too: when an agent speaks, white light lands on a dark card (ON AIR); when your mic opens, the card itself becomes light (TALKBACK). Nothing else in the product ever inverts, so the two most consequential states in a voice tool are unmistakable and hue-free — they can't collide with Raph's red or Donnie's purple. Everything else follows from that discipline: semantic state is a lamp plus a word, persona accent is identity only (avatar, spine, waveform), and the room is a density ladder — the one who's speaking gets the space, the one waiting on you gets a preview, and the four who are just working sit on a bench.

01 Tokens

Two color systems that never trade jobs

The test: delete every persona color and the room is still perfectly readable. Delete every state color and you still know who everyone is. Core four turtles own the four saturated hues; guests get earth tones, which keeps the whole reserved band — cyan, lime, pink, white — permanently free for state.

System sans for anything a human wrote or an agent said. ui-monospace for everything the machine knows: state, timers, model, path, counts, cost. That one split tells you at a glance what is speech and what is telemetry.

Space, radius, motion

4px base. Card padding 11/12, 8px between cards, 13–15 for panes, 18–22 for phone sheets. Radius is equipment-grade: 6 controls, 10 cards, 14 panes, 20 sheets. 999 is reserved for exactly two objects — lamps and the round talkback key. Round means press me.

Motion budget, hard cap: lamps breathe (1.8s), the on-air meter animates, the caption highlight advances. Nothing else moves. The avatar's 70ms frame swap is the fastest thing on screen and nothing is allowed to compete with it.

The avatar contract

Every avatar is a bare <img> in a round mask. Ring, glow, state lamp and meter are all sibling elements drawn by the parent — no filters, no transitions, no React state on the image itself — so the 70ms img.src write stays a single DOM mutation outside the render tree.

Sizes: 44 panel card · 28 bench · 40 phone card · 52 dock · 124 call screen. Placeholders on this board are initials in a persona-colored disc.

02 Surface 1 — Desktop panel · the Room

900 × 640 · the density ladder

An equal-tile grid says all six agents are equally important. They never are. But a wall of equal full-width strips has the opposite problem — four idle sessions eat the screen. So the room stratifies into three tiers by state: program (on air, expanded, captioned), attention (asking / hand up, with the preview you'd otherwise have to click for), and the bench (working, idle, muted, stale — three across, one line each). Sort is state-driven and the top slot is frozen, so nothing jumps out from under your cursor mid-click.

07 Rationale

The contested calls, decided

Four independent boards converged on a broadcast frame for the state system — that convergence is evidence, and I kept it. Where they disagreed, here's what won and why.

call 01 · on air

Light, not hue — and the mic gets the inverse

Three boards picked a hue for speaking (amber, green, teal). Every one of those eventually collides: amber sits next to Mikey's orange, teal sits next to a guest, and any saturated choice competes with four saturated personas for the same glance. So on-air is achromatic: brighter surface, white lamp, white transport, the only glow on screen.

That created a new problem all four boards had — they also made PTT white, so "he's talking" and "you're talking" looked alike. My fix is the system's spine: output puts light ON the card; input turns the card INTO light. Full inversion, used nowhere else, readable on the dock from your peripheral vision and on the phone from across a room.

call 02 · layout

Neither grid nor uniform strips — a density ladder

Equal tiles claim six agents matter equally; they don't. But full-width strips for everyone means four idle sessions eat the window. So state drives size, not just color: the speaker gets a captioned program strip, anyone waiting on you gets a strip with the preview inline, and everyone else gets a one-line bench, three across.

Sort is state-driven (on air → asking → hand up → bench) and the top slot is frozen while you're hovering, so the wall never reorders out from under a click.

call 03 · voice

Broadcast is structure; the copy is a dev room

The metaphor earns the tally lamps, the program pane and the talkback key — those are genuinely the right mechanics for "one audio channel, many sources." It does not earn the cosplay. "Transmission," "night shift," "on-air talent" and "the bridge" are all out.

ON AIR is the entire metaphor budget, spent once. Everything else is what you'd actually say: hand up · 3 unheard · release to send · cached, no spend · 2 clips are still queued. The characters supply the personality — the chrome shouldn't try to compete with them.

call 04 · interpreter

A receipt, not a status

The plan renders as: the utterance verbatim (with a one-tap "not what you said?"), then steps that each carry an outcome line, a duration, and a spend note where money was possible. Destructive steps stop the plan and ask inline with the consequence attached — never execute-then-apologize.

Plans persist as history with undo. A voice command layer you can't audit is a voice command layer you stop trusting the first time it mishears you, and it will mishear you.

call 05 · state

Every state is a lamp and a word

Color is never load-bearing on its own. Each state is a lamp plus a mono uppercase label, which survives colorblindness, a dimmed display, a 26px dock sliver, and — practically — lets a seventh state be added later without hunting for an unclaimed hue.

Room-level state is header-scale only: hazard stripes and amber live in the bar, never on a card, so "the room is held" and "this agent needs you" can never be confused.

call 06 · money

Cost is visible, quietly

Every clip costs a Gemini rewrite and an ElevenLabs synthesis. So: "cached · free" under a replay, "these don't bill" over live intermediates, "1 superseded" on a card whose queue collapsed, the model named in the picker's confirm sentence.

One mono line each, never a warning banner. A UI that hides its own cost trains expensive habits.

What I'd cut

  • CUTThe room-level transport bar. Transport belongs to a channel, not a room. It lives on the on-air card, in the dock, and in the player — three places you're already looking.
  • CUTFull transport on every card. Bench cards get a lamp and a ⋯. Six buttons across eight cards is noise, not capability.
  • CUTSwap persona and status-info as card-face controls. Both rare, both in ⋯. Status becomes a hover/long-press card flip, not another modal.
  • CUTA "current mode" label. Mode is implied by what's lit — stripes mean held, a cyan pill means live. A word that says "normal" earns nothing.
  • CUTGrid density preferences. Density is set by state, not by a setting. There is one right layout for a given room.

What I'd add

  • ADDThe unheard cue as a real object. One count in the room bar, one "catch up" on the phone: plays everything unheard oldest-first at 1.25×, karaoke sheet open.
  • ADDInterpreter history with undo. The only thing that makes a voice layer trustworthy.
  • ADD"Superseded ×1" on the card face. If an agent spoke three times and you only get the last, the wall should say so — that's a queue trust issue, not a detail.
  • ADDConsequences inside confirms. Every destructive dialog names what's lost, in clips and sessions.
  • ADDLast-spoken snippets in Resume. You remember what a session said, not where it lives.
  • ADDA frozen top slot. The wall re-sorts on state change, but never while your cursor is over it.
