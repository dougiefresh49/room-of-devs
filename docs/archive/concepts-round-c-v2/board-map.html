APPROACH The airspace over done. A concept board for Room of Devs.

Work in flight is literally in flight: every thread is a craft descending through rings of altitude toward touchdown at settled, anything that needs your hand circles in an amber holding pattern, and Mikey is the controller who owns the sky. Two seconds tells you whether the room is calm or circling for you; twenty seconds tells you exactly what's happening and how close each thing is to landing.

(one-off · light · full)
  • Position is progress. Craft spiral inward as gates pass. A thread that stalls stops descending — drift is visible before it's a problem.
  • Waiting has a shape. A hold is not a badge: it's a craft physically circling amber with your name on it. You can see it from across the room.
  • Silence is the default. Landings flash green and tick the pad counter — nobody speaks. Mikey's voice is reserved for operator gates: blocker, needs-feedback, plan-review, settled. Everything else queues for a lull (the "2 held" chip).
  • The three dials are on the glass. Ceremony = the gear column on each strip; voice = who's on comms at the console; engine (brain tier) = the flash/frontier chip plus the burn ember. Spend is ambient, not a spreadsheet.
  • Workers are drones. Silent teal rings docked to their craft. They never speak; they never appear in the strips.
  • Mikey is never swallowed. The console is his fixed anchor — the sky is something he shows you, blinking, breathing, always bottom-left.

This is the whole phone, on purpose. The owner is still deciding what mobile should be, so this round ships exactly three things: Mikey's animated face (he lipsyncs here when audio routes to the phone), one line of sky — count, plus the single amber fact if one exists — and push-to-talk.

No map, no strips, no interior views. If you need depth on the phone, you ask Mikey out loud and he answers out loud; the flattened ribbon exists only so a glance at your phone matches a glance at the desktop. The phone's job is to be a walkie-talkie with a face.

Decisions

What I chose, what I rejected, what it costs

ChoseRejectedThe tradeoff
Radial altitude map — distance from center is distance from done Free-form constellation / force graph Position always means one thing, so a glance is honest; I give up arbitrary clustering and node-drift prettiness.
Amber holding patterns for anything waiting on you Notification badges and counts Waiting gets a shape and motion you can't miss from across the room; several simultaneous holds cost some visual calm.
ATC flight strips as the only textual mirror — monospace, read-only, undraggable Cards, lanes, tables Reads like an instrument, not Trello; power users lose bulk actions the tracker itself already owns.
Spend as engine burn — per-craft flare plus one ember bar A cost table on the main surface Ambient at a glance, exact numbers one click deep in close approach; you trade instant precision for calm.
Mikey fixed at the console; the sky is what he shows you Avatar floating inside the map The character can never be swallowed by the visualization; bottom-left is permanently his.
Depth via close approach, expanding in place Drill-down detail pages You never leave the sky; very long transcripts scroll inside the overlay instead of getting a full page.
"Live" as a spoken subscription with a visible comm tether A separate live/call screen or mode One mental model — you asked, he narrates, you stand him down; there's no dedicated screen to make live feel like an event.

New engineering — one line

The daemon must compute an airspace projection into PanelSnapshot — per-thread phase, progress-within-phase, hold state and age, burn rate, plus launch/hold/land events; everything on this board is client-side rendering of that single new payload.

What I'd cut if you told me to rein it in

  1. Continuous orbital motion (per-craft trajectories synced to real progress) — the priciest idea here. Cheap version: craft sit at fixed bearings on their rings and only state transitions animate — hold pulse, landing flash, launch ping. You keep 90% of the glance value for 20% of the work.
  2. The live terminal feed inside close approach. Cheap version: a last-20-lines snapshot refreshed on each snapshot rev — no streaming channel, still feels like looking inside the machine.
  3. One-off suborbital arcs and landing-history marks. Cheap version: one-offs surface as an artifact chip at Mikey's console and landings just tick the pad counter. The arc is theater; the chip is the feature.
